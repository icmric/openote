# v1.0.3

Two things live here. First, the **proposed scope** for the release, written on 6 November
2026 for the owner to cut. Below it, **everything raised during v1.0.2 that was bigger than a
patch**, as it was written — nothing in that part is committed to this version.

Updated on 8 November 2026 for six issues raised since
([#38–#43](#raised-since-6-november)): two went into **Must**, three into **Should**, one into
**Could**, and the share-link import ([#36](https://github.com/icmric/openote/issues/36)) was
[investigated](onenote-share-link.md) and left out.

## Proposed scope

**The aim is to be ready to be looked at.** The plan on
[#34](https://github.com/icmric/openote/issues/34) is a public post to r/OneNote and a couple of
other communities around January, as people get ready for school and university. Everyone who
arrives from one does the same four things — looks at the screenshots, opens a PDF, writes or
draws, and comes back the next day — and then judges the app on whether any of them went wrong.
v1.0.3 should make those four unremarkable, and pay the two debts the issue tracker holds in
writing: [#12](https://github.com/icmric/openote/issues/12) and
[#16](https://github.com/icmric/openote/issues/16), both promised for this version.

Three tiers. **Must** is cheap, owed in public, or protects somebody's work. **Should** is what
was promised, ordered by how many new users will see it. **Could** is ranked and is cut from the
bottom.

### #38 — The undo toast never goes away

**Reported.** After deleting a page or a section the "Deleted page “Untitled page” · Undo" bar stays
on screen. The screenshot for #42 shows another one, "Deleted group “Group 2”", still up behind an
open dialog.

**Cause, confirmed.** `showDeletedSnackBar` in `app/lib/ui/sidebar.dart` is the app's **only**
`SnackBar` with an action button. In the Flutter this app builds with, `SnackBar.persist` defaults to
`action != null` — *a toast that has a button waits to be dismissed*. The other toasts have no button
and time out as before, which is why only deletes showed it.

**Fix.** `persist: false` and an explicit `duration`; an Undo should outlast a "Saved", so about eight
seconds. A widget test that pumps past the duration and finds the toast gone: the existing one
(`test/sidebar_delete_key_test.dart`) checks the toast appears and offers *Undo*, and never that it
leaves.

**Adjacent.**
- **Ctrl+Z undoes the delete and leaves the toast up**, still offering an Undo that has nothing left
  to do. Hide the toast when the node is back. (`undoNodeDelete` falls back to `restoreDeleted` when
  the stack has moved on; check that pressing the stale button then does nothing at all.)
- Deleting two things in a row already replaces the first toast (`hideCurrentSnackBar`); keep it.

### #40 — A pasted box lands on the last one

**Reported.** Copy a box, press Ctrl+V without clicking, again and again: the copies pile up. The
title says "stack"; the text says "instead of being in a singular block", which is not obviously the
same complaint — see the question under *Replies owed*.

**Cause, confirmed from the code.** `AppState.pasteBlocks` puts every copy at the copied box's own
position plus a fixed 28 px (`fresh.x = at?.dx ?? src.x + 28`). The source never moves, so the second
paste lands exactly on the first, and the third on both. When the original has been scrolled or
zoomed out of view the copy lands off screen as well.

**A second bug, certain, on the same two lines.** Given a point (`at`, which the right-click → Paste
route passes), the function sets **every** copied box to that point. Copy two or more boxes together,
right-click → Paste, and their layout is lost: they all land in one pile.

**Fix (S).**
1. Keep the group's relative layout: anchor the top-left of the copied boxes' bounding box on the
   target, not each box.
2. Step further on each repeat paste of the same clipboard (28 px, then 56…), as slide and drawing
   apps do.
3. If the original is not in view, drop the copy into the view.

**Open: where should it land?** OneNote pastes where you last clicked, and right-click → Paste here
already does. Ctrl+V could do the same when the pointer is over the page, and cascade otherwise. That
is the better behaviour, but it is a decision the reporter's answer may settle.

### #39 — Ctrl+V pastes the wrong clipboard

**Reported.** Have something on the clipboard, copy a box (the screenshot is the box's own *Copy*
menu), press Ctrl+V: what was there before is pasted, not the box.

**Why — from the code; not reproduced.** Copying a box keeps it in the app's own memory and **never
touches the system clipboard**. A canvas Ctrl+V (`_pasteFromSystemOrBlocks` in `app_shell.dart`) must
therefore guess which is newer, and does it by comparing the system's *plain text* now with a snapshot
taken at copy time (`AppState.blockClipboardIsNewer`). That works for text. It cannot work for the
other things a clipboard holds:

| What was on the clipboard before | What Ctrl+V does |
|---|---|
| Plain text, readable at copy time | The box — **correct** |
| An **image** (a screenshot, a picture copied from a page) | The image. Images keep first claim *on purpose*, so a fresh screenshot beats an old box; the box never gets a say |
| A **file** | The file, same rule |
| Text, but the clipboard could not be read at the moment of copying (Windows lets another program hold it open) | The old text. A missing snapshot counts as "not newer" |

A person who has been copying screenshots and files into their notes first is exactly who holds an
image on the clipboard, so that is the likeliest reading. **Reproduce before fixing**, from each of
the three starting points (text, an image, a copied file).

**Fix — stop guessing.** On Copy and Cut, **write to the system clipboard**: a custom Openote format
carrying the boxes, plus the plain-text form `blockClipboardAsText` already builds when every box has
one. Ctrl+V then reads what is actually there; whatever is copied afterwards replaces it on its own.
No snapshot, no "newer" test, no image-wins rule. `super_clipboard` 0.9.1, already a dependency, has
custom formats (`SimpleFileFormat`), so there is no new package. The risk is per platform — Windows,
macOS and Linux each register a custom type their own way — which is why it belongs in the hardware
pass. Keep the in-memory copy as a fallback if the clipboard write fails: *Cut* removes the boxes
first, and it must never leave nothing to paste.

**Free with it.**
- Copy a box and paste its words into any other app (Notepad, a chat, an email).
- Paste between two Openote windows, and after a restart. The clipboard today lives in memory and
  dies with the app.

**Check first — pasting a picture into a different notebook.** Blobs are stored **per notebook**
(`_blobStore(notebookId)` in `repository.dart`), and the copied JSON carries a picture's hash but
**not its bytes**. A picture, PDF or file copied in one notebook and pasted into another should
therefore point at nothing. Untested. If it is confirmed it is a bug in its own right, and the new
copy payload is the place to fix it: carry the bytes, or copy the blob across by the source notebook's
id.

### #41 — Buttons are drawn too slim

**Reported.** "The height of their hitbox is too small", throughout: the Notebooks dialog footer
(*New · Import · Repair · Get started · Done*), the Settings language drop-down and *Close*, the
navigator footer, the Insert ribbon.

**Measured on 8 November** with a throwaway widget test under the app's own theme (not committed):

| Button | Painted | Clickable box |
|---|---|---|
| `TextButton`, `TextButton.icon` — the dialogs | **20 px** tall | 40 px |
| `CommandButton` — the ribbon | **24 px** tall | 40 px |
| `IconButton` — the navigator footer | 32 px | 40 px |

**Why.** `buttonBase` in `app/lib/theme/onote_theme.dart` asks for a 28 px minimum height
(`OnoteSize.buttonCompact`), and `VisualDensity.compact` then takes 8 px off it. Material's tap-target
padding keeps the *clickable* box at 40 px, but it is invisible: people aim at the 20 px they can see.
99 places across 31 files set compact density or a shrink-wrapped target of their own, the theme's
among them.

**What is not known.** Whether the reporter is missing the *drawn* button — it looks too thin to aim
at, which is certain — or the *clickable* one, meaning some control really is smaller than 40 px. The
measured buttons pass the second reading, so the controls to test are the custom ones: the language
drop-down, the split buttons in the ribbon (*PDF slides ▾*, *Table ▾*), and the navigator footer.
That is a pass over each screenshot's controls on a real build, clicking the edges — and the question
on #41.

**Fix.** One change in the theme: stop drawing buttons at less than their stated size, so the

## PR 9, the review (12 November)

[PR #9](https://github.com/icmric/openote/pull/9), *fix: make save handling durable on exit*, by an outside
contributor, open since 11 September. 6 files, +251 −52, one commit, signed off. It merged into this
branch with no conflicts, and its own 18 tests pass.

**What it fixes, and is right about**

  * **A failed write was remembered as written** (`RustEngine.savePage` stored the page's hash before the
    write). The next save of the *same* text compared equal and was skipped, so a transient failure (disk
    full for a moment) could silently become permanent. The hash now follows the write.
  * **Two overlapping saves** (the debounce and a close, say) could finish out of order, and the older one
    cleared the dirty flag of edits made after it started. Saves now join one another and a revision
    counter keeps the flag honest.
  * **Closing now waits for the save in flight**, and the update dialog flushes again just before it lets
    an installer take the process away.
  * It also notices that a failed write of the *workspace list* (`workspace.json`) was swallowed.

**What was wrong, and is fixed in the merge.** The PR made `shutdown()` throw whenever the page was
still marked unsaved, and made the window refuse to close on a throw. Reasonable for a save that failed.
But the flag is also permanently true in two cases that are not failures: a notebook Openote is only
*showing* (its log is from a newer build, so by design nothing is written), and an edit with no page open.
As submitted, closing the window was impossible in both (**shown by a test on the PR as submitted**), and
there was no way out of a disk that never recovers except killing the process, which loses the same edit
with less warning. The merge keeps the PR's guarantee and adds: dirt that no save would keep never blocks;
a refused close is refused **once**, says why and how to leave in the status bar, and a second deliberate
close (between 1.5 seconds and a minute later, so a double-click does not count) leaves without the edit;
if the disk comes back in between, the retry just saves. 8 new tests.

**Not changed, worth knowing.** Each save now serialises the page to JSON and back once more than before
(the PR does it so the engine and the op-log recorder see the same snapshot while the user keeps typing);
for a very large page that is extra work per save, not measured. Installing an update calls `shutdown()`,
which also switches off the housekeeping timers for the rest of that session if the install then fails.
Neither seemed worth changing.

## A download whose file never changes (Windows)

Asked: a fixed `Openote-Setup.exe` for every version, so downloads build
reputation with Microsoft and Defender stops flagging it.

**What the reputation is attached to.** SmartScreen ("Windows protected your
PC") judges an *unsigned* file by its **hash**, so each release is a new,
unknown file and starts again from nothing. That is the owner's recollection and
it is right. Two consequences:

- **A fixed file name changes nothing.** The name is not what is looked up. An
  `Openote-Setup.exe` alias for the same bytes as `openote-1.0.3-…-setup.exe` is
  cosmetic.
- **A licence changes nothing.** Reputation comes from download volume of one
  hash, or from a code-signing certificate. Neither is affected by the licence
  in the repository.

**Two things are being called "Defender".** SmartScreen is a reputation check
applied only to files that arrive with the browser's "Mark of the Web". Defender
Antivirus is a scanner that reads the file's contents and behaviour, and flags
by heuristics (a freshly built, unsigned installer that writes to Program Files
and runs a silent child process looks like a dropper to it). The fixes differ,
and only the first is addressed by anything below.

**Option A — a signing certificate (the real fix, already in the section
below).** Reputation attaches to the *certificate*, so every future release
inherits it and no stable file is needed. Azure's Trusted Signing service
(about US$10 a month; check current eligibility for an individual and for the
owner's country before relying on it) signs from CI without a hardware token.
This ends the per-release reset, which is the whole problem.

**Option B — a stable downloader.** A small `Openote-Setup.exe` whose bytes
**never change**, which asks the GitHub API for the newest `-setup.exe` and its
`.sha256`, downloads it, verifies it and runs it. Two real effects:

1. All downloads of the *downloader* count against one hash, so its reputation
   can accumulate across versions.
2. The installer it fetches is written by a program, not a browser, so it has
   **no Mark of the Web and SmartScreen never examines it**. (The in-app updater
   already gets this for free, and the new checksum check is what makes that
   safe.)

Costs and risks, stated plainly:

- It must be **byte-identical** across releases, so it is built once and
  re-attached, not rebuilt by each release run (a rebuild changes timestamps
  and so the hash). The release workflow would copy it from a fixed place.
- Reputation still starts at zero and takes a large number of downloads and
  weeks; there is no guaranteed threshold.
- **Downloaders are the shape of file antivirus distrusts most.** An unsigned
  tiny program that downloads and runs an executable may trigger Defender
  *more*, not less, than the installer does today. This is the main reason not
  to build it before trying A, and it can only be answered by trying it on a
  clean Windows machine.
- It can be written without a runtime (a small Rust or Inno Setup stub) or as a
  Dart/Flutter console build; the first is smaller and less suspicious, and the
  repo already has a Rust toolchain.

**Recommendation.** Do A. If signing is not possible this release, B is a
reasonable experiment, but build it only once the checksum step above has
shipped (so the downloader has something to verify), and test it against
Defender on a clean VM before publishing it. Neither is built on this branch:
neither can be exercised without Windows.

macOS and Linux are unaffected; a stable name there buys nothing, and the
version stays in their file names.

### Second pass (10 November): what the evidence supports

Researched from Microsoft's documentation and developer write-ups as they were
quoted by search results; the Microsoft pages themselves could not be opened
from the build machine, so **each claim below is only as good as its source and
should be checked at the link before money is spent on it.**

**Corrections to the first pass.**

- *"Reputation attaches to the certificate, so every release inherits it"* was
  too confident. Microsoft's documentation describes **two signals**, the
  file's hash and the signing identity, and sources disagree about how far the
  second carries a brand-new file. One reading: signing every release with the
  same identity lets the certificate's reputation carry forward. Another,
  quoting Microsoft: a new signed binary still gets a prompt until *its* hash
  has history. Treat signing as helpful and not as a guaranteed end to the
  warning.
- *"An EV certificate gets immediate trust"* is **out of date**. EV and OV are
  now treated alike by SmartScreen; EV's bypass was removed in 2024. Do not pay
  more for EV for this reason.
- A renewed or replaced certificate is a new identity and resets whatever it
  had built, so the certificate should be one that can be kept.

**Free signing for open source: SignPath Foundation.** This is probably the
free certificate the owner remembers. It signs open-source projects from CI
under the Foundation's name (so the publisher shown is SignPath Foundation, with
a required credit line). Conditions reported: an OSI-approved licence (the
AGPL qualifies), a public repository, and builds produced by a public CI
workflow (which `release.yml` is). It is an OV-level certificate, so it gives
a verified publisher and a stable identity, **not** instant SmartScreen trust.
Worth applying for: it costs nothing and removes "Unknown publisher". Eligibility
and the exact terms are on signpath.org.

**Paid options.** Azure's Artifact Signing (formerly Trusted Signing) was
reported as open to organisations in the USA, Canada, EU and UK and to
*individuals in the USA and Canada only*, and needs a paid Azure subscription.
That probably excludes an individual in Australia; confirm before relying on it.
A commercial OV certificate is the fallback.

**Why other projects show no warning.** Not established from a source, so these
are the likely reasons rather than findings: very large download counts mean
each new file builds history within hours; a long-lived signing identity;
distribution through channels that never attach the browser's Mark of the Web
(winget, Chocolatey, Scoop, the Microsoft Store, in-app updaters); and, for the
biggest, direct relationships with Microsoft. A project with 60 to 80 downloads
a release is simply below the volume where the hash alone earns trust, and
Microsoft publishes no threshold.

**Which warning is it?** The fix depends on it, and "flagged and blocked" can
mean three different things:

| What is seen | What it is | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Blue "Windows protected your PC", *More info ▸ Run anyway* | SmartScreen reputation | Signing, download volume, per-release review request |
| The browser says the file "isn't commonly downloaded" or blocks it | Edge/Chrome reputation check (Safe Browsing / SmartScreen) | Same, plus Google/Microsoft site review |
| Defender removes it as a named threat (`Trojan:Win32/…`) | An **antivirus detection of the contents** | Submit the file as a false positive to Microsoft (the Security Intelligence file-submission portal); change whatever triggers it |

**Next step for the owner:** when it happens next, record which of the three it
is and, for the third, the exact threat name. Nothing in the packaging looks
unusual: `openote.iss` needs no administrator rights, installs per-user, and
runs no scripts. The app does start `git`, and its update path starts the
installer, but nothing launches a shell or touches the registry beyond the file
associations.

**Testing without a new physical machine each time.** Reputation lives in
Microsoft's cloud, keyed by the file, so a machine that has already run the
installer is not a fair test only because of *local* state: a "Run anyway" you
already chose, and Defender's local history. Repeatable options, best first:

1. **Windows Sandbox** (Windows 11 Pro/Enterprise): a throwaway clean desktop on
   every launch, with its own Defender. Download in Edge inside it, the real
   path a user takes.
2. **A virtual machine with a snapshot**, reverted before each test. Works for
   the same reason. Give it internet and leave cloud-delivered protection and
   sample submission on, since a VM with those off is not representative.
3. **Testers' own machines**, for the genuine article, but only once per file:
   after they click through, that file is no longer a first-time test.

I could not find a source on VM-specific differences in Defender's behaviour, so
the first VM result should be compared once against a real machine.

**Windows channels that skip the browser entirely.** Submitting to the
[winget](https://github.com/microsoft/winget-pkgs) repository (free, a manifest
that points at the GitHub release and carries its SHA-256, and can be automated
from the release workflow) gives Windows users `winget install` with no
browser download, and so no Mark of the Web. The Microsoft Store (a one-off
registration fee for an individual) delivers a Microsoft-signed package. Both
are worth a look *beside* signing, and neither needs the installer's name to
stay fixed. The stable-downloader idea from the first pass remains the weakest
option because Defender is least trusting of exactly that shape.

**macOS.** The $99 a year is the only route to a clean first launch. Apple's
fee waiver exists but is for non-profit organisations, schools and governments
with a legal entity, and excludes sole proprietors, so it does not fit.
Unsigned remains usable with the one-time `xattr -cr` already on the download
page. Revisit if donations ever make it payable.

## Updating on Linux without downloading a file

Investigated, not built. Installed forms today are a `.deb`, an `.rpm` and a
`.tar.gz` (the AppImage was dropped on purpose: no menu entry or icon, see the
header of `packaging/linux/build-packages.sh`). The dialog explains why Openote
does not install them itself (`ui/update_dialog.dart`). The options, with what
each one cannot do:

| Option | Result | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Publish an apt and a dnf repository** (static files on GitHub Pages or R2, signed with a project GPG key) | The system's own updater offers Openote beside everything else; **no in-app code and no elevation by us**. Best fit for how Linux users already work | A signing key to create and keep safe; a workflow step per release to regenerate the indices; one-time "add this repository" instructions on the download page |
| **`pkexec` + the package manager** from the dialog (`pkexec apt install ./openote.deb`) | One click, with the desktop's own password prompt. Works for people who installed the `.deb` or `.rpm` | The dialog must know which they installed and the distro's tooling; a failed install must not leave the old one broken; the same checksum check applies. Reasonable people dislike an app that elevates |
| **Self-replacing tarball** | No root | Nothing records where the tarball was extracted or that it was one; replacing the wrong directory breaks an install. A marker file written at build time would fix the "which install" half |
| **Bring back an AppImage with zsync delta updates** | In-app, no root, small downloads | Reintroduces the no-menu-entry problem that removed it, unless the AppImage integrates itself on first run |

**Recommendation:** the repositories. It is the only option that needs no
privileged code in the app, it is how a Linux user expects software to update,
and the owner's point stands that this audience can manage a manual download in
the meantime. The updater's current behaviour (open the download page) is a
fine default until then.

### Second pass: running the Linux repositories

- **Effort:** a signing key, a workflow step and an afternoon of work for the
  first one; the RPM side is quicker. Hosting on GitHub Pages is free and fits a
  low-volume project; two jobs writing to the same Pages branch collide unless
  runs are serialised, which one write-up solved with concurrency controls.
- **Automatic from a GitHub release:** yes. The step runs in the release
  workflow after the packages are built, regenerates the indices, signs them
  and publishes; nothing is done by hand per release. Only the one-time key
  creation and setup is manual.
- **Do users update without asking?** Not by default. Installing from the
  repository puts Openote in the normal update list (Ubuntu's Software
  Updater, Fedora's Software, `apt upgrade`, `dnf upgrade`), the same as any
  package. Fully unattended updates are off by default for third-party
  repositories on Debian and Ubuntu: `unattended-upgrades` must be told the
  repository's origin. That is their choice to make, and it is the right
  default.
- **Shipping the repository with the package:** the `.deb` and `.rpm` can drop
  the repository definition and key as part of installing, which is how Chrome
  and VS Code do it. Someone who installs the downloaded package once is then
  on the update path for good.
- **Telling how it was installed:** the app can ask the system, for example
  whether `/opt/openote/openote` is owned by a package (`dpkg -S`, `rpm -qf`),
  or the package can include a marker file. If so, the update button should stop
  opening the website and instead show the one command
  (`sudo apt update && sudo apt install --only-upgrade openote`, or the `dnf`
  equivalent) with a copy button, and try to open the desktop's software
  updater. Opening that updater is not uniform across GNOME, KDE and others, so
  the command is the part that always works.

## Definite sums and integrals

Asked: *"feasability of solving equations with large/complex operators
(integrals, summation, limit, etc)?"*

**The first piece of work is not evaluation at all.** A `\sum_{i=1}^{n} i` from
the palette loses the operator and both limits on the way to the calculator:

```
stored tex : \sum _{i=1}^{n}i
linear     : "§i"
calculator : unexpected "§"
```

`math_linear_projection.dart` emits a placeholder for a script whose base it
cannot speak, and the same is true of `\int`, `\lim` and `\prod`. So carrying the
construct across that seam comes first.

After that, in increasing order of difficulty and decreasing order of
advisability:

1. **A definite sum or product.** `∑_{i=1}^{5} i²` = 55. Tractable, and the
   parts exist — `compileFormula` already compiles a body into a closure over a
   map of variables, so the index binds in a loop. Needs the projection fixed, an
   integer-limit check, and a cap so a mistyped limit cannot hang the UI isolate.
2. **A definite integral.** `∫₀¹ x² dx` ≈ 0.3333 by Simpson's rule over the same
   closure. Comparable work, plus a convention for the `dx` — which the
   prefilled template writes, and which the parser would have to read as a bound
   variable marker rather than two letters multiplied.
3. **A limit.** Approachable by sampling towards the point, and **not worth
   doing.** Indeterminate forms, one-sided limits and false convergence each
   produce a confident wrong number, and a limit that is quietly wrong is worse
   than one that declines to answer.

(1) and (2) make the Evaluate block genuinely useful for a calculus problem set
and neither touches the vision's non-goals. (3) and a step-by-step walkthrough
are both reversals of a stated decision, for different reasons.

## The Evaluate block, inline in a sentence

Asked: *"Difficulty of enabling solution box to be inlined with text and other
elements?"*

**Moderate, and the storage half is already done**, which is the surprising
part. `InlineAtom` holds a thing that is not text inside a text block, and it was
built to take more than one kind: its `type` is a string rather than a
`BlockType` so that an atom of a type this build has never heard of survives
being read and written back, and the payload is a block's own content shape. So
sync, undo, cut/copy/paste, the op log and every export need **no format work at
all**. `table` is simply the only value in use today.

What it costs is the dispatch, and the sites are known: the renderer in
`inline_atom_view.dart` (the real work), stepping into it with an arrow key in
`live_markdown_engine.dart`, the read-only view in `text_block_view.dart`,
Markdown export in `md_common.dart`, PDF export in `pdf_vector_export.dart` in
two places, the label in `history.dart`, and insertion — Evaluate with the caret
in a sentence should make an atom rather than a block.

Plus one real refactor: `SubstituteBlockView` reads and writes `block.content`
directly and would need a binding instead. `InlineTable`'s `TableBinding` is the
template and `blockAtomHost` is the bridge that already exists.

**The focus machinery is the part that would have been hard and is not any
more** — an atom holding `TextField`s inside a paragraph is exactly what
`inline_table.dart` solved, and `core/focus_claim.dart` means a field inside a
paragraph no longer loses the caret.

This is [v1.0.2 #3](archive/v1.0.2.md#3-one-way-to-hold-a-piece-of-content)'s principle
applied to one more type, and the two are cheaper together than apart.

## Flashcards, rebuilt as a box

**Decided.** Asked on GitHub ([#16](https://github.com/icmric/openote/issues/16)):
multi-line cards, with lists, code, images and formulas — *"would be incredible for
review sessions"*. The owner's decisions:

- **Edited directly on the card**, not in a dialog: *"more intuitive, clean, and
  shows actually what it looks like rather than the user just guessing. It also
  keeps it aligned with the rest of the app."*
- **Each side is, more or less, a regular box**, so it behaves the same and has the
  same abilities: *"i dont want to have to maintain a seperate maths, code, and
  image handler just for flashcards."*
- **Maths first.** Code and images if they are not much harder; tables only if they
  come easily.
- **Markdown export: the raw Markdown of each side**, open to suggestions (below).

### Why rebuild, rather than extend `?[front](back)`

The format is one line of Markdown with brackets for edges, which rules out
multi-line by construction and makes code, images and display maths fight the
delimiters. And it is wrong today, not only limited — measured during v1.0.2:

- **A card's schedule is its line number.** `Flashcard.id` is `blockId:line`, and
  only tagged lines are carried across when lines move. One line typed above two
  cards hands the first card the second's schedule. v1.0.2 bug 16 is the stopgap;
  this is the fix.
- **A `)` in an answer is not a card at all.** `?[Derivative of x squared](f(x) = 2x)`
  yields zero cards, silently. MCP's `create_flashcards` works round it by rewriting
  `(` to `[` in every answer it writes.

### The shape: an inline atom whose two sides are text

The inline table already solved "an object that is not text, living in a
paragraph", and a card fits it almost exactly. The reference stays in the
paragraph's text — `![Flashcard: What is osmosis?](onote://atom/<id>)` — and the
payload sits beside it in the same block:

```jsonc
"atoms": {
  "<id>": {"id": "<id>", "type": "card",
           "content": {"front": "<markdown>", "back": "<markdown>", "w": 420, "h": 160}}
}
```

What that gives for nothing, because the table needed it first:

- **A permanent id**, so the schedule follows the card through any edit.
- **No delimiters.** The sides are JSON strings, so brackets are only characters.
- **Each side is the app's own Markdown dialect** — the same text a box holds — so
  every renderer that draws a box draws a side: inline and display maths, code
  fences, `![](sha256:…)` pictures, lists, multi-line.
- **A size of its own**, `w`/`h` in the payload. This was v1.0.2's "a flashcard has
  no size of its own", which needed a format decision in the Markdown line; here it
  needs none, which is what the inline table's column widths were pointing at.
- **Sync, undo, cut/copy/paste, `read_page` and `append_blocks`** already carry atoms.
  An older build keeps one it does not understand and shows its alt text, which is
  a better failure than today's.

### Editing on the card: two boxes, not two handlers

The requirement — *a side is a regular box* — is also the cheapest way to build it.
**Each side is a real paragraph editor**, hosted in the card the way an inline
table hosts its cells, so a side gets maths, code, pictures, the toolbar, the
shortcuts and spell check from the code that already does them for every box, and
anything a box learns later a card learns with it. `inline_table.dart` is the
template: a `TableBinding` lets an atom's content be read and written like a
block's, `blockAtomHost` bridges it, and `core/focus_claim.dart` is why a field in
a paragraph no longer loses the caret.

Two questions specific to cards:

- **Flip versus edit.** A tap turns a card over today. Editing a side has to win
  without losing the flip: probably edit the side you are looking at, with the
  turn on its own control — as the block form already does with its edit button.
- **Tables inside a side** — the nesting the owner allowed *"if it's easy"*. A side
  being a box makes it possible; a table in a sentence in a card in a sentence is
  the first three-deep case the focus machinery would meet. Decide once the rest
  works.

### Moving existing cards across

Convert `?[front](back)` lines to card atoms **on page open**, the way tables were
converted — `table_conversion.dart` proves the result before it writes and refuses
rather than half-converting. **Carry each schedule from `blockId:line` to the new
id at the moment of conversion**, the one moment both are known. The old
`BlockType.flashcard` blocks (`blockId:0`) convert the same way.

### Markdown export

Raw Markdown, as suggested, with one refinement so it reads as a card everywhere:

- **A card whose two sides are single lines of plain text** exports as
  `?[front](back)`, as today — so it comes back into Openote, and into older
  builds, as a card.
- **Anything richer** exports as a `<details>` block with the question as its
  summary and the answer's raw Markdown inside:

  ```markdown
  <details><summary>What is the discriminant?</summary>

  $$\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$$

  </details>
  ```

  GitHub, Obsidian and most Markdown viewers render that as click-to-reveal, which
  is the nearest thing Markdown has to a flashcard, and every one shows the answer's
  Markdown as Markdown. A multi-line *question* does not fit a summary, so a card
  with one exports both sides inside, under bold "Question" and "Answer" labels.

### What it costs, and what to build it beside

Bigger than a patch release. It touches the same places as [the Evaluate block,
inline in a sentence](#the-evaluate-block-inline-in-a-sentence) — the renderer,
stepping in with an arrow key, the read-only view, Markdown and PDF export, the
history label, insertion — so the two are cheaper built together than apart. On
top of those: the study panel drawing a rich side (it uses `inlineSpans` today,
inline formatting only), the conversion, and MCP (`create_flashcards`, the block
schema, `read_page`).

## Version-history rollback

Only if the [version-history decision](archive/v1.0.2.md#decisions-taken) goes to building
rollback over removing the surface (decision 2 above). Recorded so the design does not have to be rederived.

**Rollback needs no new storage.** The op log is a complete, ordered, never-
compacted history of every block that has ever existed, and `block.set` carries
the whole block with its device, sequence, Lamport clock and timestamp. A page
as it stood at any past moment is a replay of the log up to a time. What is
missing is the projection and the UI, not the data.

1. **Replay to a point.** `SyncRecorder` already replays the whole log to build
   `Materializer` state; replaying to a bound is the same code with a predicate.
   The work is making it cheap enough to do interactively on a big log — the
   same problem `warmRecorder` already solved once by moving a replay off the UI
   thread.
2. **Show it per block.** `block_authors` already holds device, Lamport, seq and
   `changed_at` per live block, so the per-paragraph attribution asked for is
   largely computed already. What it cannot show is what the block used to
   *say*; that comes from the replay.
3. **Put it back as an ordinary edit** — write the old content as a new
   `block.set`. **Never rewrite the log.** Rolling back is appending the past,
   not deleting the present, which keeps one writer per file and means a
   rollback syncs like any other change and can itself be undone.

Two caveats. **A rolled-back page can reference bytes that are gone** once the
forget op exists, so rollback and collection have to agree: a forgotten image
restores as the stand-in, not as a hole, and the UI should say which. And
**granularity is block-level**, which has been accepted — *"even if its just per
paragraph or whatever"* — so word-level blame is off the table.

## Hand a copy of something to somebody

> *"It would be nice to be able to export a copy of a page, section, section
> group, or entire notebook and share it with someone. Its a copy frozen in
> time, however being able to email or otherwise send it to someone could prove
> helpful. Would ideally need to be something which the receiving user could
> just double click and it auto populates a new notebook with the content."*

One file, emailable, that the recipient double-clicks. A frozen copy rather than
a shared notebook: no sync, no device registry, no way for either side to see
the other's later edits.

**Most of this already exists.** A page's content is Page JSON; a section or a
group is a `nodes` subtree plus the pages under it; and blobs are
content-addressed, so the set to include is whatever those pages reference.
`export/open_export.dart` already walks exactly that and writes it to a folder.
What is missing is a single-file container and an import that reads one.

Four things to settle, and the first is the real decision:

- **What the receiving end does with it.** The ask is "auto populates a new
  notebook", with a preference for not creating one. Those pull in opposite
  directions: importing *into* an existing notebook needs a target chosen, and a
  rule for what happens when it collides with what is there. Creating a new
  notebook needs nothing and is honest about being a copy. **Recommend: create
  one, say so, and offer "put it in an existing notebook instead" as a second
  step** — which is also the easier half to build and the one that cannot go
  wrong in a way that loses somebody's existing page.
- **Every id is minted fresh on arrival.** Data model §2 rule 3 already requires
  it for paste and import, and it is what stops a shared copy from colliding with
  the original if it ever travels back. Links *inside* the shared selection are
  rewritten to the new ids through the same id-map; links pointing outside it
  become dangling, which is correct and should render as such rather than being
  silently dropped.
- **It is not a `.onotebook`.** A notebook folder carries a device registry and
  op logs, and handing those to somebody else means handing them a device
  identity. A share file should carry content and nothing about who wrote it —
  which is also what makes it safe to email.
- **Say what travels and what does not.** Ink and pictures travel, because blobs
  are included. A page window pointing at a page that was not shared cannot, and
  the recipient should see a tombstone saying so rather than an empty box.

Worth shipping beside multiple-notebooks-open below, since dragging between two
open notebooks is the same operation without the file.

## Several notebooks open at once

Raised on GitHub: OneNote keeps more than one notebook open in the sidebar, and
Openote opens exactly one. Two reasons to want it, and the second is the stronger
one: seeing two notebooks at once, and **dragging a page or a section from one to
the other**, which today means export and re-import.

**One notebook open at a time is load-bearing in more places than it looks.**
`AppState.notebookId` is a single value that the canvas, the sidebar, the search,
the planner, the study deck and every recorder lookup read. Opening a second is
not a sidebar change; it is making "the current notebook" a property of what you
are looking at rather than of the application.

What that touches, as a scoping sketch rather than a plan:

- **The recorders already are per notebook** (`_recorders` is a map keyed by
  notebook id), which is the half that would have been hardest and is done.
- **The container handles likewise** — `Repository` holds one per open notebook
  already.
- **`AppState.notebookId` is the problem.** Every read of it has to become "which
  notebook is this widget showing", and the sidebar has to render more than one
  tree.
- **Cross-notebook drag needs the id-minting rule above**, because a page moved
  between notebooks is a copy-then-delete unless both notebooks agree about ids,
  and they do not.

## Drag a page out of Openote as a PDF

> *"I would like to be able to drag and drop a page, section, or section group
> into another application and have that turn it into a pdf before pasting it
> into said application. So i could drag and drop a page directly into here or
> an email and have it be readable by anyone."*

Dragging **out** of a Flutter desktop app is the unknown here, and it should be
answered before anything else is designed: `super_drag_and_drop` is the package
that claims it, and whether it can offer a file promise — where the receiving
application asks for the bytes only once the drop lands — decides the shape.
Without a promise, every drag would have to render the PDF up front, which for a
section group is seconds of work on the chance somebody drops it somewhere.

The rendering half needs nothing new. `export/pdf_vector_export.dart` already
produces a searchable vector PDF of a page, and printing already walks a
selection of pages. So this is a platform-integration question wearing a feature's
clothes.

Two things to decide once the drag mechanism is known: what a drag onto a
**file manager** should produce versus a drag into an **email** (probably the same
PDF, but Windows and macOS describe those differently), and whether a drag with
no PDF-capable target falls back to plain text or refuses.

## Signing the builds, and the warnings on all three platforms

Flagged by the owner for investigation, not designed. Three different
problems wearing one word.

### Windows — "Windows protected your PC"

SmartScreen, and it is about **reputation**, not identity. An unsigned
installer always shows it. An OV certificate (~$200–400/yr from a reseller,
and since mid-2023 it must live on a hardware token or in a cloud HSM, which
complicates CI) starts at zero reputation too and earns its way out over
downloads and time — so the warning fades rather than disappearing on day
one. An **EV** certificate gets immediate SmartScreen trust and costs more
again.

"The Microsoft developer program" is a separate thing and worth being precise
about: a Partner Center / Microsoft Store developer account is a one-off ~$19
and does **not** sign anything for you. What it buys is a route into the
Microsoft Store, and a Store-distributed app is signed by Microsoft and has
no SmartScreen prompt at all. That is a real option here — the app is already
MSIX-able in principle — but it means Store packaging, Store review and the
Store's own update channel instead of ours.

**Checked on 12 November, for an individual in Australia.** (From Microsoft's pages and community answers;
confirm before paying for anything.)

  * **Microsoft's own cloud signing service** (Trusted Signing, now **Artifact Signing**): its quickstart lists
    Australia, but for *organisations*; **individual developers must be in the United States or Canada.**
    Community answers also say new sign-ups are limited to organisations with a few years of history, and
    individual onboarding is paused (unconfirmed). So: probably not available to you as a person. The
    fallback Microsoft itself names is an ordinary OV certificate from a commercial authority.
  * **Reputation, either way:** with no signature it belongs to each file, so every release starts from zero,
    however far apart they are. With a signature it belongs to the signing identity and carries from one
    release to the next, as long as the certificate does not change. EV certificates no longer give instant
    trust. Release as often as the work warrants; **signing, not cadence, is what changes the warning.**
  * **A free option I had not mentioned before: SignPath Foundation**, which signs open-source projects at no
    cost. Its conditions, as published: an OSI-approved licence (AGPL is), a public repository, an automated
    build (`release.yml` is), no proprietary components, two-factor sign-in for everyone on the project, and
    the standard "free code signing provided by SignPath.io" credit. **The catch:** the certificate is issued
    to the Foundation, so the publisher Windows shows is *SignPath Foundation*, not Openote or you. Whether
    SmartScreen then treats a SignPath-signed file kindly I could not confirm from their pages. Worth
    applying for: it is the only route here that costs nothing and needs no company.
    **Timing (unofficial; SignPath publishes none):** one guide reports approval of the application in "a few
    days"; another project is still waiting on theirs. For each release, the conditions say a **trusted member
    of the project's own team approves every signing request**, so that step is the owner clicking Approve
    on a request from the release workflow, minutes, not a wait on SignPath. Ask them before planning a
    release around either.
  * **Not free, but not SmartScreen:** verifying the app's publisher with Microsoft (decision 3) is free and
    is about the *sign-in screen* for the OneNote import, not the download warning. The Microsoft Store is a
    one-off fee, signs for you and has no SmartScreen prompt, at the cost of Store packaging and review.

### Linux — "the developer is unknown"

Not the same class of thing, and not something to register for. There is no
central identity to apply to. What the message means depends on where it came
from:

  * **GNOME Software / Discover installing a local `.deb` or `.rpm`** says it
    because the package is unsigned and from no configured repository. The fix
    is a signed repository (an apt/dnf repo with a GPG key we publish) — then
    it is trusted the way any third-party repo is, and `apt`/`dnf` update the
    app, which is also the answer to "why is there no in-app update here".
  * **A Flatpak** would be the other route: published on Flathub, signed by
    Flathub's own infrastructure, sandboxed, and updated by the system. For a
    desktop app this is probably the lowest-friction Linux answer, and it
    costs a manifest and a review rather than money.

Either way: a build-side gap, not a missing registration, and the fix is a
distribution channel rather than a certificate.

### macOS — the $99, and whether there is a way round it

There is no free certificate that removes the warning. Apple issues Developer
ID certificates only to paid Apple Developer Program members, and since
Catalina an app also has to be **notarised** — uploaded to Apple, scanned,
and stapled — before Gatekeeper will open it without the "cannot be opened
because the developer cannot be verified" dialog. Notarisation needs the same
membership.

So the honest answer to *"surely not every little app pays"*: the ones that
do not are distributed the way this one is, and their users right-click ▸
Open, or run `xattr -d com.apple.quarantine`, the first time. That is the free
path, and it is what the download page should explain rather than pretend
away. Homebrew casks are the other common dodge — `brew install --cask` does
the quarantine removal for the user — and getting listed there costs nothing
but a PR to homebrew-cask.

**If any of this is done, do the cheap ones first:** a Homebrew cask and a
Flathub manifest cost time rather than money and remove the friction for the
two platforms where it is worst, before spending anything on Windows.

## Self-hosted sync on Cloudflare D1 and R2

A sync target that is not somebody else's Drive folder, hosted per user on a
free tier. Sketched, not designed.

The shape that fits the existing transport: ops are append-only rows keyed by
`(device, seq)`, which is what makes one-writer-per-file work and translates
directly to a table with that primary key. Pulling is "rows after my watermark",
which is one indexed query.

**Blobs go in R2, not D1.** D1 is SQLite with a row-size ceiling and a database
ceiling; blobs are content-addressed and immutable, which is exactly what object
storage is for, and R2 has no egress charge.

**The real cost is setup, not money.** A free tier covers a student's notebook
comfortably. What it does not cover is a person who has never seen a terminal
creating an account, a database, a bucket and an API token. Anything in this
direction has to answer that before it answers anything about schemas — the
reason the current transport is "point it at a folder you already have".

Still to decide: authentication (a token in the workspace, as MCP does, or
something per-device), whether a worker is needed at all or the client can talk
to D1 directly, and what happens when a free tier is exceeded mid-edit.

## Web AI clients

Spec 14 §8's table says ChatGPT's website and the Gemini app cannot reach a
local server because their connectors run server-side. That is still true for
the website, and the **desktop** apps are a different question — the ChatGPT
desktop app now reads `~/.codex/config.toml`, which Openote already writes.

**Not yet confirmed by a human: that the desktop app picks the entry up.** It

Microsoft Copilot is the worst fit of the three — its extensibility story is

and neither of which can see `127.0.0.1`.

**The zero-setup answer most people would actually use** is not an MCP client at
all: it is the user pasting a page into whatever chat they already have. Worth
remembering before building anything here, because the ceiling on this work is
how many people own a desktop AI client *and* will connect it.

## The purge test that fails on windows-latest

*In plain words (12 November).* Openote has a safety test: if you **delete a notebook from one computer**
that is synced through a shared folder, the **other computer's copy must be left alone**. The test sets
that up, takes a photograph of every file in the shared folder, performs the delete, takes another
photograph, and checks nothing changed. On the automatic Windows build, one file in the shared folder
sometimes grew by 447 bytes between the two photographs, so the check failed. It never failed on Mac or
Linux, and it passes on Windows when run alone, so it looks like **a background job from earlier in the
test writing to that folder at an unlucky moment**, not the delete itself touching it. It has been
"fixed" three times by waiting longer, and each time it came back. The job is to **find which writer
adds those bytes** (the log says it is an `.oplog`, and the two "recorded 2 nodes" lines just before it
are the first suspects) instead of waiting longer, because the test guards against a real and serious
bug (deleting a notebook on one device deleting it everywhere) and must not be skipped or retried until
it turns green. It matters for the release because a red build is the first thing a new contributor sees.

### Found and fixed, 12 November

**Reproduced** by running the test over and over while every core was kept busy: 5 failures in 12 runs, each
the same `GREW … 1489 → 1936 B`. **Traced** by logging every append to every log, with the call that made it,
beside the moments the test takes its two photographs. In a passing run device A's second save (`block.set #6`,
the text "A wrote this" being recorded again once the notebook has moved into the shared folder) lands

## The theme catalogue (#12)

[PR #22](https://github.com/icmric/openote/pull/22) from setzor is the registry
step, based on master before v1.0.2. Reviewed; worth taking after a rebase onto
the released master, with three changes:

- `themesForMode` reads the platform brightness and collapses both slots onto
  one chosen appearance. With a third entry that drops the student's choice: a
  True Black picked while the OS is light leaves Night ink in the dark slot,
  and the root's memoisation means it never recomputes. Resolve each slot from
  the chosen appearance directly — no mode, no platform read. Probed both ways
  with two and three entries.

  green through that bug. Assert identity.

## Seasonal decorations

Small, time-limited touches — a hat on something in the fortnight before
Christmas, gone a few days after — driven by the device clock, not shipped and
withdrawn by separate updates. A separate layer on top of whichever appearance
is in use, not an entry in the appearance list. Read the date through the
injectable clock the tests already use, never `DateTime.now()` in a widget, or
the suite behaves differently in December. Which occasions, and whether there
